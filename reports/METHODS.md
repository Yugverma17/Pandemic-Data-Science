# Methods, assumptions, and limitations

The README reports what was found. This document records the decisions that
produced those findings, and the reasons a reader might discount them. Every
number in the README is generated by `scripts/run_*.py` and written to
`reports/tables/`; nothing is transcribed by hand.

---

## Study design decisions

### Why the forecast target is a 7-day average, not daily counts

The forensics stage shows 145 of 184 countries with a statistically significant
day-of-week reporting effect, several with amplitudes above 2.0. Forecasting raw
daily counts therefore means forecasting which weekday a health ministry clears
its queue. That is a real phenomenon and not the one anybody needs predicted, so
the target is the 7-day trailing average at `origin + h`.

A consequence worth naming: this makes a seasonal-naive baseline degenerate —
"the value one season ago" collapses onto persistence — so it was dropped rather
than shipped as a duplicate row.

### Why deaths, not cases, are the causal outcome

Confirmed cases measure testing capacity as much as infection. A country testing
ten times more finds more cases at identical true prevalence, and testing volume
is itself a consequence of policy, which puts it on the causal path. Deaths are
far from clean but are much less elastic to surveillance effort.

Excess mortality would be better still. It is present in the OWID panel for only
~3% of country-days, mostly weekly or monthly, which is too sparse for the
epidemic-time-aligned windows this design needs.

### Why windows are indexed to epidemic time

Every treatment and outcome window starts at each country's 100th recorded case
rather than at a fixed calendar date. Comparing Italy's March 2020 with New
Zealand's compares a country three weeks into an outbreak against one with almost
no cases — a difference in epidemic *stage* that would be read as a difference in
policy.

This also fixes the case count at `t = 0` by construction, removing the most
obvious channel of reverse causality. It does not remove the one that actually
bites (see below).

### Why the capacity benchmark is a region's own prior peak

Published ICU bed counts are unreliable, rarely comparable across countries, and
say nothing about staffing — a bed without a trained intensivist is not capacity.
A level a region has already sustained is a defensible floor for what it can
sustain again, and requires no data the analysis does not already have.

The ranking, however, is by projected census **per 100,000**, not by the ratio to
that benchmark. The ratio is the more intuitive question and the wrong one: a
region going from 0.1 to 2 patients per 100k tops a ratio ranking while needing
no help at all. The ratio is retained as a severity qualifier and suppressed
entirely when the prior peak is below 1 per 100k.

## Assumptions, and what breaks if they are wrong

| Assumption | Where used | If violated |
|---|---|---|
| Serial interval Gamma(4.7, 2.9) | R_t estimation, renewal projection | R_t is biased multiplicatively; wave *timing* is preserved, so rankings survive better than levels |
| Fixed infection–hospitalisation ratio | Cases→ICU chain | Breaks after vaccination. **Measured**: median r falls 0.94 → 0.56 |
| Residual exchangeability | Conformal intervals | Coverage falls below nominal. **Measured**: 95% intervals cover ~85% |
| No unmeasured confounding | DML estimate | E-value 2.18 quantifies the strength needed to explain it away |
| Donor pool untreated in the post-window | Synthetic control | **Violated at 21 days**, which is why that analysis is reported as a null |

The pattern is deliberate: each assumption has a measurement attached rather than
a reassurance. Two of them fail, and the failures are in the README.

## Known limitations

**Complete-case attrition is not random.** The causal sample drops 27 of 175
countries for missing covariates, and poorer countries are missing covariates
more often. The estimate is an effect *among countries with complete data*, which
skews richer. It is not a global estimate and is not described as one.

**Reported COVID deaths in India are substantially under-recorded**, and the
degree varies by state. The Karnataka/Maharashtra/Delhi gaps are therefore lower
bounds on the real differences, and their *ordering* could in principle be
altered by differential under-recording — which is exactly the kind of
state-varying data-quality problem the forensics stage detects, but the state-level
series are too short to score reliably.

**The identification probes are one-sided.** They can reveal confounding but
cannot demonstrate its absence. A probe restricted to case growth *before* the
policy window returns a null for a trivial reason — at the 100th case nearly every
country still has a short, noisy series, so there is nothing for the test to
detect. The concurrent probe, outbreak size *during* the window, is the one with
power, and it fires (p = 0.032). Both are reported, because a null from an
underpowered test is not evidence of anything.

**The panel GBM is refit only every 56 days.** A model refit at every origin
might perform better. This is a compute trade-off, stated rather than hidden; the
refit schedule never uses data after the forecast origin, so it costs accuracy
and not validity.

**The refutation suite uses a lighter learner than the headline estimate** — 150
trees and 2 cross-fitting repeats, against 400 and 20. It refits the estimator
roughly 136 times, and at full size the stage ran over twenty minutes, which is
long enough that people stop running it. The trade-off is the right way round:
refutations ask whether an estimate *moves*, which tolerates far more Monte Carlo
noise than the point estimate itself. The headline coefficient is unaffected.

**Benford non-conformity is the weakest of the seven detectors** and carries the
lowest weight (0.05). A series can fail it for innocent structural reasons — a
short series confined to one order of magnitude, or a hard reporting cap. It
flags a country for investigation; it is never evidence of fabrication, and the
report says so wherever the figure appears.

**Forty countries, not all of them.** The backtest covers the 40 largest
epidemics. Skill on small-epidemic countries is unmeasured, and the
quality-versus-skill correlation (rho = −0.673) is estimated on those 40.

## What would be needed to answer the causal question properly

The cross-country design fails because policy responds to the epidemic
contemporaneously. Fixing it requires variation in policy that is unrelated to
local severity:

1. **Staggered adoption with matched pre-trends** across many sub-national units.
   Indian district-level data with genuinely staggered restriction dates is the
   most promising route, and would support a modern difference-in-differences
   estimator robust to heterogeneous treatment timing (Callaway–Sant'Anna or
   Sun–Abraham; the two-way fixed-effects estimator is biased under staggered
   adoption with heterogeneous effects).
2. **A regression discontinuity** at an administrative threshold — a rule that
   triggers restrictions at a specific case rate assigns treatment sharply near
   the cutoff for reasons unrelated to anything else about the district.
3. **An instrument** for policy timing: a plausible candidate is the timing of a
   neighbouring jurisdiction's announcement, which shifts local policy through
   political pressure without directly affecting local transmission — though the
   exclusion restriction there is arguable and would itself need defending.

None of these is available in the country-day panels used here, which is the
honest reason the question is left open rather than answered badly.

## Reproducibility

Every stage is seeded (`config.SEED = 20200311`). Raw files are downloaded once,
hashed with SHA-256, and recorded in `data/raw/manifest.json` alongside their
URL, byte count and fetch timestamp, so any figure traces back to the exact bytes
that produced it.

Upstream sources are living datasets and continue to be revised. The manifest is
what makes a re-run comparable to this one; re-running `fetch_data.py --force`
against later revisions may shift figures in the third decimal place.

The test suite runs entirely on synthetic data with known ground truth and needs
no network access, so it cannot be broken by an upstream schema change — the
failure mode where a "passing" test suite silently stops testing anything.
